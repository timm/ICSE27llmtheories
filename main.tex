\begin{abstract}
Systematic reviews of large language models (LLMs) in software
engineering report that most work targets low-level coding tasks
(generation, repair, summarisation), with comparatively little
attention to high-level activities such as effort estimation,
project planning, or the evaluation of candidate ``laws'' of SE.
We ask whether LLMs can support \emph{software science}: the
construction, reconciliation, and stress-testing of SE theories.
We present a procedure and a set of metamorphic guardrails for
using LLMs to explore parameter spaces of SE phenomena, and apply
  it to three hypotheses: (a)~Brooks's law about how to managing hiring
  for prokects that are behind shcedule;
  (b)~the value of applying architectural patterns to 
  fix defects; (c)~and understanding contradictory results
  about 
the productivity impact of AI-assisted coding. 
  We also offer (d)~64 further hypotheses awaiting exploration with
  this methodology; and (e)~a set of procedures and metamorphic
  tests for commissioning these models. Together, (d) and (e)
  define a research programme: we invite the SE community to extend
  these 67 theories with their own concerns, both to stress-test our
  procedure and to replace folklore with a shared evidence base.
  \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{S}{oftware} engineering is rich in \emph{truisms}:
broadly-applicable principles such as ``adding manpower to a late
project makes it later''~\cite{brooks1975mmm}, ``design patterns
remediate degraded code''~\cite{gamma1994patterns}, or
``AI assistants boost developer
productivity''~\cite{github2024copilot}. Such claims are seductive
because they offer a quick basis for decisions about hiring,
tooling, and process.
The trouble is that they often fail in specific cases. 
 Menzies et al.~\cite{menzies2002truisms} used
model-based simulation to show that even canonical SE truisms
(``raise process maturity'', ``remove defects early'') were
sub-optimal in a surprising number of projects.
Devanbu et al.~\cite{devanbu2016belief} surveyed
practitioner beliefs at Microsoft and found many of them
to be founded in idiosynractic 
personal experience rather than in evidence. The current AI era
adds fresh truisms (``AI makes you faster'') which, as we show below,
are weakly supported by contradictory findings~\cite{becker2025metr}. 

The literature
on candidate SE laws is now too large for any one researcher to
re-derive in a working lifetime. We have cataloged
67 such truisms of which a sample of 20
are presented in Table~\ref{tab:theories}.
Large language models (LLMs) could automate this analysis, but
mostly operate at a different ``altitude''. Systematic reviews of
LLM-for-SE work~\cite{hou2024llm4se,fan2023llm4se} confirm the
field has concentrated almost entirely on low-level coding tasks
(generation, repair, summarisation~\cite{chen2021codex}); higher-level
tasks such as effort estimation, organisational reasoning, and the
testing of candidate SE laws receive little attention. 

This paper asks
whether the same generative machinery, scaffolded by appropriate
guardrails, can support \emph{software science}: the construction,
stress-testing, and reconciliation of SE theories. We argue
yes, that months of high-level analytical work can thereby
be compressed to days.
As a concrete demonstration (\S\ref{sec:aiwork}), we use a small
LLM-built systems-dynamics model, \texttt{aiwork}, to reconcile
three apparently contradictory studies of AI-assisted coding: a
19\% slowdown~\cite{becker2025metr}, a doubling of code
churn~\cite{gitclear2024churn}, and a 2--4\% quality
gain~\cite{github2024copilot}. 
Our models show each samples a different region of one surface
(generation speedup $\times$ churn inflation; for details see \S\ref{XXX});  % CLAUDE: keep this cross reference
none generalises.
Hence the insight here  is not a verdict on a truism but a directive
for data collection: the model identifies the per-team ratio of
speedup to churn as the single most informative next
measurement (and this is not a widely reported measure).
To say that another way,
models of this
kind can thus intelligently guide \emph{what to measure next}.

But this this work is not without risk. Once truisms are made executable,
the volume of generated data can overwhelm a researcher into
confabulating spurious conclusions---a hazard amplified, not
removed, by LLMs. We therefore
propose our method as a discipline rather than a magic wand. The
rest of this paper explains, with examples, all the follow steps
which applying it to specific SE truisms:

{\small \begin{verbatim}
    theory_exploration = sensitivity(
                           parameterSweep(
                             metamorphicGuards(
                               LLModeling())))\end{verbatim}}
The metamorphic guards (\S\ref{sec:method}) are designed to surface
hallucinated coherence before it reaches a conclusion. Nothing in
this paper should be read as a new truism replacing the old ones;
we report what \emph{this} method found on \emph{these} models, not
universal laws.

In summary, our contributions are:
(a)~a procedure with explicit guardrails for LLM-assisted SE
theory exploration (\S\ref{sec:method});
(b)~validated findings on three hypotheses---Brooks's law,
architectural patterns, and AI-assisted work
(\S\ref{sec:validation}); and (c)~a research programme grounded
in a companion catalogue of 35 explorable and 32 maturing SE
theories\footnote{\url{https://timm.github.io/icse27theories/}}.
We invite the community to extend these 67 theories with their own
concerns, both to stress-test our procedure and to replace folklore
with a shared evidence base.


\section{A Peek at the Methodology}
\label{sec:method-peek}

In our approach, intutions about softwre projects begin  life as hastily drawn
diagarms which are then formalized as systen dynamics models.
These models are then validated then executed to explore the intuitions.

For example, Figure~\ref{fig:cld} shpws an inution about the effects of adint newcomers
to a ssoftware project.
According to the diagram,
 newcomers slow down projects since (a)~they must be trained by veterans (who stop
  producing since they are training); and (b)~intro-project communication grows
  more complex because there are now $n(n-1)/2$ communcation channels.
The net result is Brooks' Law~\cite{brooks1975mmm}; i.e.
 adding staff to a late project makes it later.

Figure~\ref{fig:sd} re-draws the same
story as a systems-dynamics (SD) model. Now, the node has some semantics:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item \emph{Stocks} (rectangles, e.g.\ \textsf{Todo},
        \textsf{Done}, \textsf{New}, \textsf{Vet}) are containers that hold
        ``stuff''.
  \item \emph{Flows} (double-line pipes with a valve, e.g.\
        \textsf{prod}, \textsf{mature}) move stuff between stocks.
  \item \emph{Auxiliaries} (small circles, e.g.\ \textsf{train},
        \textsf{comm}) carry no stuff; they compute multipliers
        that open or close the valves.
\end{itemize}
Stocks adhere to \emph{conservation of matter}; i.e. the current contents
are their initial value, plus in-flows, minus outflows.
In a discrete-time format:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stock}
\mathit{Stock}(t+\Delta t) = \mathit{Stock}(t) + \Delta t \cdot (\mathit{inflow} - \mathit{outflow}).
\end{equation}

def brooks():
  # {key: [default, lo, hi]}.  UPPER-case = stock, lower-case = param.
  init = {
    'Vet':         [10,    0, 100],   # veteran devs at t=0
    'New':         [0,     0, 100],   # new hires
    'Done':        [0,     0, 500],   # cumulative completed tasks
    'Todo':        [500,   0, 500],   # remaining backlog
    'boost':       [0,     0, 100],   # *ctrl* hire surge at t=10
    'comm_coef':   [0.005, 0, 0.05],  # comm-overhead per Vet pair
    'train_coef':  [0.2,   0, 1],     # Vet hours spent per New hire
    'prod_rate':   [5,     0.1, 20],  # baseline output per Vet
    'mature_rate': [0.1,   0, 1] }    # rate New -> Vet

  def step(dt, t, u, v):       # u = state at t,  v = state at t+dt
    # comm overhead: n*(n-1)/2 pairs * coef
    comm  = u.Vet * (u.Vet - 1) / 2 * u.comm_coef
    # training drain: each New steals train_coef of a Vet's time
    train = u.New * u.train_coef
    # net production: Vet hours not lost to comm or training
    prod  = u.Vet * (1 - comm - train) * u.prod_rate
    # backlog drains; floor at 0 to prevent overshoot
    v.Todo = u.Todo - dt * max(0, prod)
    v.Done = u.Done + dt * max(0, prod)
    # New stock matures away; boost is a one-shot pulse, fires in
    # the tick containing t=10 (avoids float-equality misses).
    pulse  = u.boost if t <= 10 < t + dt else 0
    v.New  = u.New - dt * u.mature_rate * u.New + pulse
    # Vet stock grows by maturation flow
    v.Vet  = u.Vet + dt * u.mature_rate * u.New
    # params carry forward unchanged
    for p in ('boost','comm_coef','train_coef',
              'prod_rate','mature_rate'):
      setattr(v, p, getattr(u, p))
    return v                          # hand the new state back
  return init, step

from types import SimpleNamespace as ns
from random import uniform

def tri(c, lo, hi): return c + (uniform(lo, hi) - c) * uniform(0, 1)

# SD semantics in 4 lines: sample init -> repeatedly call step.
def run(model, steps=200, dt=0.1):
  init, step = model()
  u = ns(**{k: tri(*spec) for k, spec in init.items()})
  for i in range(steps):
    u = step(dt, i*dt, u, ns(**vars(u)))       # one tick: u -> u'
  return u

can be generated by applying
Equation~\ref{eqstock} to the nodes of Figure~\ref{fig:sd}.
Note that within the {\em init} block of our models
each numeric range has the form \texttt{[c,lo,hi]} (e.g.\ \texttt{'Vet':[10,0,100]}).
This 
  encodes a triangular distribution with  mode $c$, range $[lo,hi]$,
  peak probability at $c$ falling linearly to zero at the endpoints.
  Such distributions can be  sampled cheaply~\cite{SCHERER2003331} via the one-line approximation
  $x \approx c + (u-c)v$ where $u \sim \mathrm{U}(lo,hi)$ and
  $v \sim \mathrm{U}(0,1)$.

Note that this modeling process looks deceptively simple. Having
applied this to 67 truisms of softwre engineer, we can attest
that from here the work
gets much harder. That work includes assembling real-world project data
(to conduct prudence checks);
designing metamorphic tests
to catch faulty models; extensive consultatiion with domain experts to explore
valid parameter
ranges;  and deciding which questions are worth asking and which
observables actually settle them.  ALso, 
as said in the introduction, once truisms are made
executable, the volume of generated data can overwhelm a
researcher into confabulating spurious conclusions.

The rest of this paper shows
where LLMs can carry much of that load, and where they cannot.

\section{Recent Industrial Experience with AI}
\label{sec:industry}

The case for stress-testing AI truisms before adoption is no
longer academic. Across 2025--2026, several large organisations
publicly reversed AI-first strategies after measured outcomes
failed to match the marketing. A recent industry
review~\cite{siliconmoney2026aicancel} catalogues several
examples:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item \textbf{Starbucks} abandoned an AI inventory system across
        11{,}000 stores after nine months; it could not reliably
        count milk and syrup.
  \item \textbf{Microsoft} pledged \$80B for AI infrastructure
        in early 2025, then cancelled over 2\,GW of planned data
        centre capacity and restricted internal Claude Code use
        to control token spend.
  \item \textbf{Klarna} cut staff from 7{,}000 to 3{,}000 on the
        promise that an AI chatbot replaced 700 agents---then
        began rehiring 15 months later after service quality
        degraded.
  \item \textbf{Duolingo} went ``AI-first'' and phased out
        contractors; by April 2026 it removed mandatory AI use
        from performance reviews after public backlash.
  \item \textbf{Salesforce} reduced support staff from 9{,}000 to
        5{,}000 using AI, then announced hiring of 1{,}000 new
        graduates in April 2026.
  \item \textbf{Uber} had 95\% of engineers using AI tools but
        exhausted its annual AI budget in four months, with no
        measurable link between usage and shipped features.
  \item \textbf{Pizza Hut}: a franchisee operating 111 locations
        filed a \$100M suit alleging a mandatory AI dispatch
        system pushed on-time delivery from 90\% to roughly 50\%.
  \item \textbf{Air Canada} lost a tribunal case after its
        chatbot invented a bereavement-fare policy; the airline
        unsuccessfully argued the bot was a separate legal entity.
  \item \textbf{Builder.ai}---once valued above \$1B with
        Microsoft backing---collapsed after revelations that human
        engineers performed most ``AI-generated'' work, alongside
        \$180M in accounting irregularities.
\end{itemize}

These reversals are consistent with the \texttt{aiwork} reading
(\S\ref{sec:aiwork}): aggregate AI claims hide large per-deployment
variance. The cost of skipping the stress test is no longer a
methodological footnote; it is layoffs, lawsuits, and write-downs.
